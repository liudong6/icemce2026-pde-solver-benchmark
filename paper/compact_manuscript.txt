::abstract
We present a verified finite-difference heat-conduction benchmark that tests whether coefficient summaries distinguish solver choices. Matched binary fields preserve contrast, material fraction, total variation and maximum log-gradient. At contrast 1000 and grid size 192, translating a two-island field by one node raises Jacobi-PCG iterations from 403 to 677 and reverses the setup-inclusive winner in all five repeats. Source symmetry explains the avoided slow modes; a classical-AMG audit shows that decision boundaries depend on the candidate set. All 2956 controlled extension solves pass an independent residual check. Smooth and aligned-interface tests verify the discretisation, while a paired calibration quantifies callback overhead separately from historical timings. CPU/CUDA results concern stencil execution with resident GPU data. The contribution is a reproducible audit of conditional solver costs, not a new selection algorithm.

::section 1. Introduction and context
Variable-coefficient heat conduction couples discretisation accuracy to sparse-solver cost. Using SciPy, NumPy, Numba and PyAMG [[1,2,3,4]], we audit when coefficient summaries fail to distinguish the fastest verified solve. Finite differences, conjugate gradients (CG) and algebraic multigrid (AMG) are established methods [[5,6,7,8]]. Recent work addresses sparse multi-GPU efficiency and GPU PDE preconditioning [[9,10]], communication-reduced AMG and anisotropic diffusion [[11,12]], and reproducible benchmarking [[13]]. HPGMG-FV and AMG2023 already combine elliptic problems with performance measurements, including setup costs [[14,15]]; we do not claim this combination as new.

Hierarchy quality can affect AMG convergence beyond scalar contrast [[16]]. Ginkgo separates linear-operator algorithms from architecture-specific backends [[17]], while stencil and CUDA studies expose sensitivity to memory traffic and kernel design [[18,19]]. Three-precision AMG and adaptive-precision matrix-vector products further motivate explicit arithmetic settings [[20,21]]; our experiments use float64. High-contrast island modes [[22,23]], source-dependent Krylov convergence [[24]] and material-aware aggregation [[25]] supply the numerical context. Feature-based solver prediction [[26]], cheaper feature selection [[27]], multimodal solver/preconditioner selection [[28]] and online multiphysics solver choice [[29]] pursue selection policies. We instead test descriptor sufficiency with exact matched-field controls, without training a selector or benchmarking those policies.

Our contribution is the controlled connection between preserved coefficient invariants, forcing symmetry and measured setup-inclusive choices. Rigid translations preserve shape and connectivity as well as scalar descriptors; source and candidate-solver controls identify why a coefficient-only decision map cannot be reused uncritically.

Full verification tables, coefficient definitions and implementation details appear in Supplement S1–S3. At N = 128 and contrast 100, inclusion and checkerboard condition estimates differ (6.43e5 versus 3.12e4), already showing that contrast alone is insufficient.

All runs use one Windows 11 workstation (Ryzen 9 7940HS, 16 GB RAM, RTX 4060 Laptop GPU). The principal candidates are CG, Jacobi-PCG (J) and default PyAMG 5.3.0 smoothed-aggregation PCG (SA). Selection minimises fresh preconditioner setup plus solve time; assembly and source construction are excluded, as is the cost of testing other candidates. Three protocols must be distinguished [[31]]. Historical comparisons use a unit source, rtol = 1e−8, per-iteration true-residual callbacks, fixed order and first-use PyAMG import inside setup; threads and power states were not recorded. The controlled extension uses warm imports, shuffled order, one BLAS thread, paired setup seeds and count-only callbacks, with rtol = 1e−9. Calibration holds these controlled execution settings fixed at rtol = 1e−8 and changes only the callback within each pair. Zero initial guesses and zero absolute tolerances apply; extension acceptance requires an independent relative residual ≤1e−8. Timings and winner boundaries are compared within protocols, never pooled across them. Full defaults and iteration limits are in Supplement S2; environment versions are in S3.

::section 4. Matched-field, forcing and solver-set audit
Two 2-by-8 motifs have 12 high cells and 20 interface edges, but one or two connected components (Figure 1). High/background conductivities are c/1. Dilation by s = N/16 and low-conductivity padding give 12s² high nodes, mean 1 + (c − 1)12s²/N², total-variation proxy (c − 1)20s/(N − 1), and maximum nodal log-gradient √2(N − 1)log(c)/2. Total variation sums absolute nearest-neighbour differences and divides by N − 1; the log-gradient uses centred interior and one-sided boundary differences. For jumps it is a fixed-grid descriptor. These identities are checked after embedding. A one-node translation preserves them, component sizes and shape, while changing alignment with the source and boundary. Comparing different motifs alone does not isolate topology causally.

After a 576-solve exploratory screen, confirmation uses c = 1000, N = 96, 128, 192, centred/transposed/x-shifted configurations, both face averages and four unit-norm sources: constant, sin(2πx)sin(πy), sin(πx)sin(2πy) and fixed-seed Gaussian. J and SA each have five fresh paired setups. The 1440 confirmation solves, 640 solves from eight prespecified 3-by-6 motifs, and 132 translation/source-perturbation solves pass acceptance (maximum residual <3e−9). The independent family balances four connected and four disconnected patterns; it is not a random material sample. The pilot retains 84 failed acceptance checks, excluded from successful timings.

Let R reflect interior nodes in x, D = diag(A), S = D^(−1/2) A D^(−1/2), and g = D^(−1/2)b. When RA = AR and Rb = −b, the zero-start Krylov space generated by S and g is odd and orthogonal to x-even eigenvectors. The centred two-island field has two slow x-even Jacobi-scaled modes; computed squared source overlaps are <1e−30 at N = 64, c = 10000 for both face averages. Joint transposition preserves J iterations, whereas x translation breaks reflection symmetry. This is an invariant-subspace explanation, not new convergence theory.

::section 5. Stencil scaling and implications
At N = 2048, variable-coefficient stencil times are 0.1813 s (NumPy), 0.005325 s (Numba serial) and 0.003725 s (Numba, four threads); more threads did not improve the largest case. Separate resident-data Jacobi updates give CUDA/CPU speedups near 1 at N = 512, then 1.52×, 3.23× and 3.51× at N = 1024, 2048, 4096. CPU uses four threads and CUDA 16-by-16 blocks. Host-clock timings include launch and final synchronisation of 20-update batches, excluding allocation, compilation and transfers. At least three batches and 0.2 s yield within-run averages, not independent replicates. These are kernel results, not end-to-end sparse-solver speedups. Full curves and byte models are in Supplement S3.5 and S2, respectively. A four-point linear fit predicts a negative CPU time at N = 512 and cannot establish a crossover threshold.

The audit shows why forcing, alignment and candidate solvers must be checked before reusing coefficient-based choices. Physical interpretation additionally requires a justified interface law, and timing interpretation requires the callback and setup protocol. Evidence remains limited to synthetic structured 2D problems and one workstation. Descriptive correlations have overlapping resampling intervals and resolution confounding (N = 256 uses an N = 128 spectral proxy); they do not establish descriptor rankings or population confidence. Neither production robustness nor universal solver thresholds follow from these measurements.

::section 6. Reproducibility
Numerical evidence and scripts, including callback calibration, are archived at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22874266 (v5). The accompanying supplement retains complete tables, plots, protocols and failures; the present compression changes presentation only. Tests cover flux energy, matched descriptors, reflection and transposition, with explicit CPU-only/CUDA-required modes. Timing reruns remain machine-state dependent.
